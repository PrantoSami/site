    Censorship by design

    Turkey’s constitution says the press is free and shall not be censored. Then you keep reading.

    By Pranto AhadMay 2026

    The news media landscape

    On paper, Turkey’s media scene looks fine. In reality, it’s a mess. The country runs hundreds of newspapers and dozens of national TV channels, and its internet penetration sits among the highest in the region, hovering around 88.3% at the start of 2025 and pushing past 90% by the second half of the year, per DataReportal and the national stats office, TurkStat.1 But size doesn’t equal pluralism. Reporters Without Borders puts roughly 90% of national outlets under direct or indirect government control.2 And it’s not always state ownership in the strict sense, either. The reality is messier. Pro-government holding companies, Demirören, Turkuvaz, Albayrak, scooped up flagship titles like Hürriyet, Sabah, and ATV after the previous owners hit legal or financial trouble, and those papers and channels now mostly parrot President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan’s AKP almost line for line.

    The big outlets shape what most Turks actually consume. TRT, the public broadcaster, sets the official tone. Sabah, Hürriyet, and Yeni Şafak dominate print and digital on the pro-government side. On the opposite end, smaller and often financially shaky outlets like Sözcü, Cumhuriyet, BirGün, plus broadcasters such as Halk TV, Tele1, and Now TV, try to fill the gaps, with international players like BBC Turkish, VOA Turkish, and Deutsche Welle Turkish picking up the rest, mostly online.3 What gets covered? Erdoğan, mostly. Always Erdoğan. After that: the lira, inflation, the Kurdish conflict, earthquakes (especially after the 2023 disaster), migration, and Turkey’s balancing act between NATO, Russia, and Syria. What rarely sees daylight: corruption stories, the wealth of Erdoğan’s inner circle, and any serious look at how state institutions actually run.

    How press freedoms operate

    Technically, press freedom in Turkey is guaranteed. Article 28 of the Constitution flatly says the press is free and shall not be censored. Then you keep reading, the next paragraph, the next law, the one after that, and the guarantee basically evaporates. Censorship works through a tangle of penal code articles, anti-terror statutes, and a 2022 “disinformation” law that makes “publicly spreading misleading information” punishable by up to three years behind bars.4 Journalists routinely get hit with Article 299 (insulting the president) and Article 301 (insulting the Turkish nation or its institutions). Meanwhile, the broadcasting watchdog RTÜK slaps critical TV stations with massive fines and broadcast bans, while the Press Advertisement Institution (BIK) starves opposition papers by cutting off state ad revenue.5

    Compared with the United States, the gap is glaring. The First Amendment treats government interference with the press as a baseline constitutional violation. New York Times v. Sullivan (1964) made it almost impossible for public officials to win defamation suits against journalists without proving “actual malice.” In Turkey, a single tweet about Erdoğan can put a reporter in pretrial detention by sunrise. There’s no Sullivan equivalent. No real shield law. Internet censorship is close to systematic, thousands of sites and social accounts get blocked every year, and platforms like X have repeatedly throttled bandwidth inside Turkey to fall in line with takedown demands.6

    The risks reporters face aren’t abstract. They’re physical. Journalists covering protests get tear-gassed, beaten, or hauled off on the spot. Critical reporters deal with dawn raids, asset seizures, travel bans, and prosecutions framed as “terrorism” or “disinformation.” CPJ’s annual prison census has consistently put Turkey near the top of the worst-jailers list, and as of 2025 more than a dozen journalists were locked up for their work.7 Foreign correspondents fare only a little better. Press accreditation can be pulled with no explanation, visas denied, and reporters, including those working for major Western outlets, have been deported, detained at the border, or, like Deutsche Welle’s Alican Uludağ in February 2026, detained in their own homes.

    A specific case: Alican Uludağ

    On the evening of February 19, 2026, police showed up at the Ankara home of Alican Uludağ, a court reporter for Deutsche Welle’s Turkish service, and took him in. The charge: “insulting the president” under Article 299, tied to his social media posts and his coverage of politically charged trials.8 Uludağ isn’t some fringe blogger. He’s a veteran court reporter, the exact kind of journalist a working democracy needs sitting in the back of a courtroom. He was filing for a German public broadcaster, not running an underground operation. None of that protected him.

    What really stings about the case is how unremarkable it has become. Article 299 has been used against thousands of people since Erdoğan came to power, including children, opposition politicians, and reporters whose supposed “crime” was paraphrasing a leaked document or quoting a critic. In April 2026, CPJ, RSF, and 22 other press freedom groups put out a joint statement condemning Turkey’s pattern of weaponizing the disinformation law and the insult laws against journalists, and called for both to be repealed.9 Turkey shrugged.

    Uludağ’s arrest is the whole story in miniature: a working journalist, a foreign-funded outlet, a vague speech crime, an evening raid. This isn’t censorship by accident. It’s censorship by design.

    Update, October 2026

    Uludağ was released under judicial supervision at his first hearing on May 21, after 90 days in pretrial detention. On July 21, police detained him again over a post criticizing the judiciary. A court released him about a day later and barred him from leaving the country.

    Shi-losophy

    The modern man gets a few free minutes of philosophy a day, in the bathroom, on the mornings he forgets his phone.

    By Pranto AhadFebruary 2025

    First published on Medium.

    The modern average human occasionally forgets to take his phone to the bathroom with him in the morning. Then the sorcery of revolutionary philosophy unfurls within him and lasts for some insignificant minutes until he is done and is expected to return to his pragmatic world. Unlike the modern man, ancient Greek philosophers such as Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle got to spend their entire existence inside a bathroom where the walls were made out of security and comfort and it would shower privilege and inherent resources. Their thinking would be questioned but their freedom to think would not.

    Then, perhaps a shiny bathroom never unfurled that freedom inside the modern man. It was the fourth sin that never let him realize he might not be hanged for defying god.

    Sacrilege

    By Pranto AhadApril 2025

    First published on Medium.

    My desire of her runs deep, deeper than the depth of my soul. I want her but want is an insufficient word to unfurl what i crave, for, i crave and regret every breath we make separated. But togetherness or mere her presence will not bring calm to my raging desires. I shall continue exploring her presence and my eyes need to bleed until I cannot look at anything else but her maroon eyes. But looking at her till eternity is not enough. I need to experience her smile, that sharp smile that is sharp enough to obliterate admirers certainly enough to validate my existence. But all that is not enough! I used to believe my want of her is infinite until of course; I realized my love for her is infinite yet superior. But how can that occur, how can my infinite unconditional love be grander than my infinite desire for her to be mine. That is when I discovered mathematically, thematically and rationally that one infinity can be bigger than the other despite both containing endless run. It’s like a parallel line moving endlessly without any object to stop them, however! At all times, one line slightly stays ahead of the other. I love her and i love her a little more than I want her. God somehow made a slight miscalculation I guess, when he created us. She did not have to bother creating worlds and be all powerful to be adored and respected yet i made her his equal! the goddess she deserves to be, for I sometimes or at all times, worship her endlessly, INFINITELY more than I ever obey him.

    The other side

    A poet asks the people on the other side what it feels like to be loved.

    By Pranto AhadJanuary 2024

    First posted on Instagram, January 2024.

    Poet’s agony, jealousy and hopelessness

    People on the other side! Tell me, how does it feel to be loved How does it feel, To know someone is keeping all your sweet memories saved, To know you have someone to hug. People on the other side! Please tell me, how does it feel to be their beloved? How does it feel, To know with them you can cherish all the pretty roads you paved, To know you have someone to hug.

    Poet’s realization and acceptance

    People on the other side! I know! I know, you tried multiple boats and Went through numerous droughts, But you could conquer all your what if thoughts, And us loners never learned to wipe our drops I know, you hid all your sufferings and Patiently tributed your god offerings, Now we bow down to offer our humble greetings, As us loners failed to learn the art of waitings.

    Poet’s message to his companions

    I know it’s easier to kneel and suffer Giving Up the last bit of hope, I know it’s easier to breathe in hate Surrendering ourselves to die with loathe. But the fragrance of love is worth every ounce, So have faith on this poet and live for once.

    Poet’s plea

    So let’s hope, all the paths we traveled don’t end in vain, Let’s pray, our love grows, lasts and stays truly divine Thus let’s wish, the peace we once wished to attain We get blessed to own and be blessed to retain.

    Poet’s last takeaway

    It’s hard to face your present pain But your future begs your patience. You and I shall keep going. We may cry, fall and fail But we will love, live and celebrate. Cause it’s hard to embrace your misery But the upcoming love, is worth the wait.

    ALIENATED

    A novel about a Nepali family’s first years in New York, and the generations after.

    Synopsis

    A father and his thirteen-year-old daughter arrive in New York from Nepal with almost no money and a cousin’s living room to sleep in. He delivers food by bicycle. She watches the strangers on the street and can’t understand why she’s supposed to copy them. Between them is the mother who didn’t make the trip, remembered as pure light.

    ALIENATED follows the family across three generations, ending with a grandson leaving home at seventeen. Each narrator reports exactly what happened. None of them reads it right.

    From Chapter Two: Hansukh

    Naturally, someone would assume that it’s hardest to deliver in summer, for the temperature. But what they don’t understand is that what’s worse is winter.

    It’s freezing cold, you wear two layers of gloves, and even then, the hands, your fingers, the fingers of your foot, are frozen. For the first 30 minutes, you feel pain. Excruciating pain. But there’s a beauty to the pain. It is that after a while, they go numb. Numb to the point that they stop feeling. You don’t feel anything.

    If I had to force myself to see the light in this dark world, it would be that when I finally do get home, I can put my frozen fingers into a sink of hot water. And after 15 minutes, my daughter would walk by and hug me from behind. And say, how was your day?

    Excerpt from a draft in progress.
